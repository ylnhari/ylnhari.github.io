Practice sessions

First sitting · 105 minutes

People leadership

15 min select evidence · 6 × 15 min practice
6 practice questions

Use 15 minutes per question. Take 5 to choose evidence, 2 to speak, 5 for follow-ups and 3 to revise.

15 minutes to select evidence. Choose real situations where you can recall the stakes, your decision, what someone else owned and what you observed afterwards. Retrieve a decision record, review, handoff or other evidence if available. Use an approximation only if you can explain its basis.

How do you lead while remaining hands-on?

What they’re testing. Can you lead delivery while contributing technically?

  • Pick one delivery. Which implementation or design choice did you personally own, and why did it need your involvement?
  • Who could decide architecture, scope, release or formal people matters? Clear decision rights let engineers act independently.
  • Retrieve a design decision, work split or handoff showing what another engineer owned and could ship or operate.

Follow-ups. What did you stop doing yourself as the team grew? Which decision could the engineers make without you?

Check. Name one direct technical contribution and one decision another engineer could make.

Tell me about guiding an engineer who was stuck or new to the work.

What they’re testing. Can you identify the blocker and develop the engineer’s judgment?

  • What had the engineer tried, and where exactly were they stuck? Consider unclear goals, unfamiliar work and blocked dependencies.
  • Which question, review, pairing session or delegated investigation did you use, and why did it fit?
  • Recall an actual artifact or observation showing what the engineer owned next and what improved.

Follow-ups. How did you know you had diagnosed the real blocker? What would you change if they needed the same help again?

Check. Show the step they could handle independently afterward.

How would you handle underperformance?

What they’re testing. Can you address a pattern fairly and follow through?

  • What did you observe against an agreed expectation? How would you hear their perspective in private?
  • Could skills, capacity, dependencies or priorities explain the gap? What support, observable goal and review date fit?
  • Who documents the agreement and owns formal decisions with Human Resources? If you lack a formal case, connect a real coaching example and label the proposed steps.

Follow-ups. What if you discover that unclear priorities caused most of the problem? What if the agreed improvement still hasn’t happened at the checkpoint?

Check. Specify a fair goal, how to observe it and who owns formal follow-through.

How do you delegate without losing quality?

What they’re testing. Can you transfer ownership while managing material risks?

  • Recall an outcome you delegated. Which interfaces, acceptance checks and decisions belonged to the engineer?
  • Which checkpoint covered a hard-to-reverse change? Risk-based review focuses attention where impact or recovery cost is highest.
  • What did you do when a blocker appeared? Look for a later decision, release or handoff the engineer handled.

Follow-ups. Which failure would make you intervene directly? How did you know your review was developing independence?

Check. Name the guardrail, the engineer’s choice and evidence of independence.

Tell me about a disagreement over design, priorities or a delivery date.

What they’re testing. Can you influence a decision across teams under real constraints?

  • Choose a real disagreement. What mattered to each group? Which quality or safety limits held?
  • Which two options were feasible, what did each sacrifice and who had the final decision?
  • What did you bring to the discussion? Retrieve evidence of the choice and its observed consequence.

Follow-ups. What would you have done if the decision owner chose the other option? What evidence would make you reopen the decision?

Check. Make both positions intelligible and show how your action affected the decision.

How have you built a team or improved its delivery capability?

What they’re testing. Can you develop capacity beyond a single project?

  • What skill or coverage gap affected delivery? Recall what you changed through staffing, interviewing, onboarding or feedback.
  • If you interviewed, which job-relevant criteria did you use and how did interviewers compare evidence?
  • How did you transfer knowledge or build maintainers? Distinguish team growth, direct reports and formal hiring or appraisal authority.

Follow-ups. Which skill would you hire for and which would you develop internally? What was your part in evaluating or supporting the people involved?

Check. Connect an actual intervention to a supported change in capability.

First sitting · 75 minutes

ML and agent platform design

15 min master answer · 6 × 8 min follow-ups · 12 min redraw and repair
7 practice questions

Set 15 minutes for the first design sketch and discussion. For each follow-up card, use 2 minutes to sketch, 2 to speak, 3 for probes and 1 to revise.

You inherit a blank slate. How would you build an ML and agent platform used by several teams?

What they’re testing. Can you turn a broad mandate into a platform that teams can adopt and operate?

  • Clarify intended use (who relies on the system and why), access, peak demand, latency, cost and consequences of errors. Pick one bounded use case and a baseline.
  • For a hypothetical ticket-triage pilot, trace a classifier training run and one signed-in request for current procedures. Where would a withdrawn procedure or uncertain write stop the workflow?
  • Which services should be shared, which belong to use-case owners, and who owns security, operations, evaluation and fallback?
  • Which pilot measures of quality, adoption, task time and support burden would justify adding another team?

Follow-ups. What would you deliberately defer? How would you decide that the pilot is ready for a second team?

Check. Name why each service exists, who owns it and the fallback for a specific failure.

What would the platform share, and when would you use a queued job?

What they’re testing. Can you separate shared policy from isolated execution and recover long-running work?

  • Clarify tenant boundaries, deadlines, workload size and side effects. Decide whether the caller can wait or needs a durable asynchronous job.
  • Control plane sets identity and tenancy rules, policies, quotas and a catalog of allowed configurations. Execution or data plane runs workloads and handles scoped storage and telemetry.
  • Checkpointing saves state for resume. Idempotency keeps repeats from duplicating effects. Reconciliation checks authoritative state after an uncertain write. How do cancellation and spend limits work?

Follow-ups. What should happen if a worker dies after a write? Who can cancel a shared job and what happens to the work already done?

Check. Name the saved job state and the evidence needed before retrying a write.

How does a model go from training data to production and improve over time?

What they’re testing. Can you reproduce, release and improve a model?

  • Ask who owns the training data, when labels arrive and which inputs are available at serving. Trace one model from data and features to evaluation, deployment, monitoring and a retraining decision.
  • Lineage records where data and artifacts came from. Offline–online parity keeps feature definitions and time semantics consistent across training and serving.
  • An experiment tracker records run settings and results. A registry, such as MLflow’s, versions model artifacts. A feature store shares and serves feature values. An orchestrator schedules and retries dependent steps.

Follow-ups. How would you reproduce the model served last month? Where can training and serving diverge even when both jobs succeed?

Check. Name the source, feature and model versions you would verify.

How would you release and evaluate an agent stack across teams?

What they’re testing. Can you evaluate changes to agent behavior and enforce action authority?

  • Clarify what the agent may read or change. Identify the model, prompt, bounded tools, trained adapter, search index and policy that must be released together.
  • Measure task outcome and trajectory, the observed sequence of steps and tool results. Use deterministic rules for policy and a model judge, checked against human judgments, for qualities such as relevance.
  • Which writes require approval of the exact action? Could one agent share context and tools, or would specialists justify the cost and latency of handoffs through better access or context isolation?

Follow-ups. What if the answer is correct but the agent used an unauthorized tool? When does a specialist agent justify an extra handoff?

Check. Describe a test where the final answer looks good but a tool action fails policy.

What breaks under load, and how would you keep teams operating?

What they’re testing. Can you find bottlenecks, contain failures and maintain a usable service?

  • Clarify peak mix and response targets. Allocate latency across checks, queues, data reads and inference.
  • Admission limits cap new work. Backpressure defers or rejects excess load. Warm capacity reduces startup delay. Isolation limits impact from heavy jobs or tenants.
  • Name retry limits and when to restore a compatible release or use a human fallback. Monitor system health, input data, task quality and business outcomes separately.

Follow-ups. How do you tell a slow model from a saturated queue? What can an operator do when the model is suspended?

Check. Name the measurement that triggers containment and the one that confirms recovery.

How do you prevent cross-team access and make actions auditable?

What they’re testing. Can you enforce current access and record consequential actions?

  • Authentication verifies identity. Authorization checks permitted reads or writes. Where do you scope retrieval to the user and verify the current revision before evidence enters model context, including cached evidence?
  • At dispatch, where are current permission, target version, exact approval and the supporting document revision rechecked? Hold a write if supporting evidence is withdrawn or materially changed. How are worker credentials scoped?
  • Audit records reconstruct who proposed, approved and executed an action and which versions applied. Debug logs diagnose errors. What should each retain, and who can see it?

Follow-ups. What happens when a document is revoked after retrieval? Can an approval for one target revision authorize a later write?

Check. Point to the service that blocks restricted evidence and the service that can block an unauthorized write.

What would you build, buy or defer, and how would you prove adoption?

What they’re testing. Can you make adoption and operating cost part of the investment decision?

  • Which existing service could support the first pilot? Compare integration effort, fit, deployment constraints, operating cost and switching cost.
  • Which authorizations, evaluations and outcomes must remain accountable internally? Identify a budget owner for each use case.
  • A supported delivery path gives teams a tested way to release, monitor and recover. Cost per successful task includes allocated platform spend, review and rework against tasks that meet the standard.

Follow-ups. Which signals justify moving off a managed service? What if a second team’s requirements break your shared assumptions?

Check. Give an adoption measure and a condition for revisiting the build or buy decision.

Second sitting · 30 minutes

LLM adaptation, training and serving

3 × 10 min practice
3 practice questions

For each question, take 3 minutes to sketch a decision, 2 to speak, 4 for follow-ups and 1 to revise.

When would you use prompting, retrieval or fine-tuning?

What they’re testing. Can you choose an intervention and compare it fairly?

  • Is the gap unclear instructions, changing knowledge or stable behavior? Compare a prompt baseline, request-time retrieval and fine-tuning on a held-out set.
  • Leakage gives training or tuning information unavailable for real unseen cases. Split by entity or time when records can overlap.
  • LoRA freezes base weights and trains small low-rank updates. QLoRA trains adapters on a lower-precision frozen base to reduce memory.

Follow-ups. How would you detect overlap across the split? Why would you still retrieve current documents after tuning?

Check. Name the data split, metric and evidence for selecting an approach.

How would you choose a distributed training setup?

What they’re testing. Can you match a training setup to a real bottleneck?

  • Clarify model size, sequence length, available memory, interconnect and throughput.
  • Data parallelism processes different examples on model replicas and synchronizes updates. Tensor parallelism divides an operation across devices, sharing compute and memory at a communication cost.

Follow-ups. Which measurements would show that communication limits scaling? How would you compare throughput while keeping optimization and quality comparable?

Check. Sketch what each device holds and what information crosses between devices.

Open the optional diagram.

Why does LLM serving slow down, and what would you roll back?

What they’re testing. Can you identify the serving bottleneck and restore a compatible release?

  • Ask for input/output lengths, concurrency, latency targets and release changes.
  • The key/value cache stores attention keys and values for previous tokens. Longer or more concurrent sequences need more memory. Compare queue time, time to first token, generation speed and memory pressure.
  • Which base model, adapter, tokenizer, prompt and policy versions must be tested or restored together?

Follow-ups. What would distinguish slow generation from queueing? What if the previous adapter expects a different base model?

Check. Name a signal that confirms the cause and the compatible bundle you could restore.

Second sitting · 30 minutes

Operating production ML

3 × 10 min practice
3 practice questions

For each question, take 3 minutes to sketch or choose signals, 2 to speak, 4 for follow-ups and 1 to revise.

The API is healthy but predictions are wrong. How do you investigate?

What they’re testing. Can you locate wrong predictions across data, features and models?

  • Identify the affected time and segments. Compare source events, feature computations and the input and model actually used at serving. A feature is an input derived from data.
  • Event time is when something happened. Processing time is when the pipeline handled it. Late events or a backlog can leave valid but stale feature values.
  • What fallback protects users while data is repaired? How would you verify what serving reads after replay or correction?

Follow-ups. How do you tell stale data from training-serving mismatch? What happens when the serving store receives a late correction?

Check. Name an affected period, a containment owner and proof of fresh served features.

Open the optional diagram.

How do you tell whether a live model is helping when labels arrive late?

What they’re testing. Can you measure usefulness while outcome labels are delayed?

  • When are outcomes available and how will you match them to predictions? A proxy provides early evidence but can vary for other reasons. Evaluate mature cohorts when labels arrive.
  • Precision is the share of flagged cases that were correct. Recall is the share of relevant cases caught. Which error matters by segment?
  • Drift is change in data distributions or input–outcome relationships. What evidence distinguishes a data shift from worse model quality? Assess incremental business impact against a baseline or controlled comparison.

Follow-ups. What would you do if an early proxy worsened but mature labels looked stable? Which metric matters if missing an important case is costly?

Check. Say what evidence you have today and what needs mature labels.

How would you set a serving reliability target and plan for peaks?

What they’re testing. Can you translate user needs and peak demand into testable targets?

  • Clarify latency, error tolerance, peak demand and fallback. A service-level objective (SLO) sets a measured target over a window, such as the share of requests finished within a threshold.
  • Synthetic example. 360,000 requests per day average about 4.2 per second. A sustained 80 per second at a mean 0.25 seconds in the system implies about 20 requests in flight on average. Test bursts and latency tails separately.

Follow-ups. Which counter tells you whether a bottleneck is queueing or computation? What would you roll back if an old model expects a different feature schema?

Check. Distinguish throughput, latency and in-flight work. Name a fallback compatible with current features.

How would you determine which quality requirements apply to a procedure assistant?

What they’re testing. Can you classify a function before committing to controls?

Agent architecture and investigation

Agent: a model-driven process choosing steps or tools within limits. Tool: a bounded function exposed to the model. Provenance: where an observation came from. Trajectory: the sequence of decisions and tool results in a run.

Follow one investigation end to end

A request supplies a symptom, permitted systems and a time window. Validate it and check which capabilities the caller may use. Create run state, then let the model select from scoped tools. Code checks arguments and permissions before execution; results return with their source, query and time range. The model continues until it can support a conclusion, needs missing evidence or reaches a limit.

For a service timeout, compare caller, service, dependency and infrastructure evidence. Align timestamps and identify the deployed revision before blaming a change. Metrics show impact; logs and traces help localize it; code explains a possible mechanism. A mechanism is not established merely because suspicious code exists. Return an unresolved finding when evidence does not settle the cause.

Single agent, multiple agents and guardrails

A single agent with parallel tools can keep one coherent investigation history. Multiple agents help when tasks have distinct access boundaries, specialist context or independently testable outputs. They also add duplicated work, inconsistent findings, coordination failures and cost. Require structured evidence from each specialist and one reconciliation step; agreement between models is not proof.

Guardrails are enforced checks, not only prompt instructions: least-privilege credentials, scope allowlists, bounded queries, result caps, redaction, retries/timeouts and model/tool/time budgets. Treat logs and retrieved documents as untrusted data. Read-only tools still create confidentiality and resource-consumption risks.

Evaluate the answer and how it was reached

A quote found in a retrieved result establishes provenance, not correct interpretation or causation. Check resource/time alignment, contradictory evidence, omissions and unsupported confidence. Use expert-reviewed incident cases, deterministic checks for critical constraints and repeated runs. If a model judges results, compare its judgments with human review. Code coverage and a fluent report do not establish diagnostic accuracy.

Worked case · Errors rise after a release

A serving API starts returning errors during a rollout. A code diff shows that the new revision lowered a dependency timeout. What observation would weaken the hypothesis that this change caused the errors?

  1. Choose the comparison. Request a permitted window covering normal traffic before the rollout and the affected period. Use error rate with request counts for context. Group by region and running revision to avoid mistaking a shift in traffic mix for a regression.
  2. Make one bounded read. Query a read-only telemetry tool for request and error counts by region and runtime revision. A structured result records the source, query parameters, time range, service, revision, counts and coverage gaps. Check release records and loaded configuration to see which revision and settings actually served traffic. Matching a quoted result to the source verifies where it came from. Verify that the measured service, revision and timestamps match the failing requests before interpreting it.
  3. Explain the contradiction. Errors were low before the rollout. During it, both revisions show errors in one region and the new one stays healthy elsewhere. A regional dependency issue could explain the overlap. The available counts cannot distinguish that from an interaction between the dependency and the new timeout. Rollout timing alone establishes correlation. Check matched traces or ask the dependency owner for timing evidence if your permissions end at the serving API.
  4. Choose the smallest investigation architecture. Use one agent loop with scoped tools while the evidence fits in one investigation. Delegate independent questions only if separate permissions or expertise justify the coordination cost. Reconcile results by region, time and revision.

Sample explanation. I’d report that errors coincided with rollout and affected both revisions in one region. That weakens a revision-only explanation. The timeout change is plausible, but I’d leave cause unresolved until the dependency owner can compare timings for failed and successful requests.

Platform design and secure retrieval

Control plane: identities, configuration, policy and release decisions. Execution plane: workers running models, retrieval and tools. RAG: retrieval-augmented generation using permitted source material. Idempotency: repeated requests do not cause duplicate business effects.

Start with use and consequences

Clarify users, decisions, data sensitivity, latency, availability, scale and failure consequences. Compare ordinary search or a deterministic workflow with an agent. Choose one bounded use case and define a useful outcome and a human fallback before building a catalogue of frameworks.

Open this topic’s diagram.

Build both retrieval paths

The document path ingests authoritative revisions, parses/chunks them, preserves source identifiers, attaches access and retention metadata, embeds them and publishes a versioned index. Withdrawal, deletion and changed permissions need an update path. The query path resolves user access before retrieval, combines appropriate lexical/vector search, reranks candidates, fetches only authorized evidence and generates with source references and uncertainty.

Similarity is not authorization. Filtering a restricted passage after it has entered the model is too late. Cached answers need a compatible access scope, source/index versions and revocation policy. Evaluate retrieval relevance separately from answer support: a generator cannot repair missing evidence reliably.

Design effects and recovery deliberately

A proposed write passes deterministic schema, authorization, target-version and approval checks. Record its intent and idempotency key before dispatch. A timeout can leave the outcome unknown; reconcile against the target system before retrying. Approval must match the exact action and relevant data version. A model fallback does not inherit authority to change the action.

Session state supports a task; long-term memory persists information across tasks and needs a purpose, access controls, correction/deletion and freshness rules. Start without it unless necessary. Measure grounded task completion, unsafe actions, latency, cost and adoption. Keep incident ownership and a tested rollback or suspension path.

Worked case · A withdrawn procedure and a requested write

In this hypothetical platform, an operator asks for an older procedure and then asks the assistant to mark a business-system task complete. Where should the request stop, and how would an independently approved write recover from a timeout?

  1. Check identity, permission and revision. The application verifies the operator’s identity and uses it to filter retrieval. The retrieval service checks each candidate against the authoritative document’s current access policy and effective revision before adding passages to model context. When the old revision is withdrawn and no permitted replacement is found, state that evidence is unavailable and direct the operator to the document owner.
  2. Check the write independently. Completion requires approved evidence. Without it, leave the task unchanged. If evidence later becomes available, record the proposed target, expected record version and exact change. The approval service checks the requester, evidence and required approver. At dispatch the executor checks the exact approved change, the supporting revision, the requester’s current authority and the target version. If the procedure was withdrawn or materially changed, hold the write and seek fresh approval based on current evidence.
  3. Resolve ambiguous completion. Before dispatch, store the approved operation and an idempotency key, a stable identifier the target can use to recognize repeats. After a timeout, the target might already have applied the write. Look up the recorded operation or verify the result with the target. If still unresolved, hold and escalate. Retry the same operation with the same key only if it remains authorized and the target supports safe replay.

Sample explanation. The retrieval service checks document access and whether the revision is active. The executor separately checks authority to change the task. I’d keep the timeout pending until the business system confirms the outcome, because a missing response could follow a successful update.

LLM fine-tuning and serving

LoRA: low-rank adaptation of a frozen base model. QLoRA: adapter training with a quantized frozen base. Prefill: process input tokens. Decode: generate output tokens step by step. KV cache: attention keys/values stored for active sequences.

Choose the adaptation method

Begin with a prompt-only baseline and a task-specific evaluation. Retrieval suits changing source knowledge and citation needs; fine-tuning can adapt stable classification, extraction or generation behavior. They can be combined, but each change needs evidence. Do not assume fine-tuning fixes stale knowledge or that retrieval automatically produces a grounded answer.

LoRA learns a low-rank update ΔW = BA while the base matrix W stays frozen. Rank and target modules affect adaptation capacity and parameter cost. QLoRA reduces base-weight memory, but activations and optimizer state still matter. Choose learning rate, sequence length, batch size and rank through comparable experiments; vendor benchmarks are not measurements of a particular workload.

Explain distributed training precisely

Data-parallel workers train replicas on different data shards and synchronize gradients. Model/tensor parallelism divides parts of a model; optimizer-state sharding addresses another memory cost. Gradient accumulation changes effective batch size. More GPUs may expose storage, preprocessing or communication bottlenecks. Compare throughput and time at the same dataset, sequence length, effective batch and quality target.

Prevent leakage with appropriate time/entity splits and a held-out evaluation set. Inspect important error classes and label quality, not only aggregate accuracy. A release needs the compatible base model, tokenizer, adapter, task configuration, data reference and evaluation results. Test adapter loading and output contracts before promoting an immutable version.

Reason about serving rather than GPU count

Budget memory for weights, KV cache, workspaces and fragmentation. Longer inputs, outputs and concurrent sequences consume capacity. Continuous batching can improve utilization while changing queue delay. Observe time to first token, per-token latency, queueing, errors and GPU memory/use. CPU utilization alone may miss the limiting resource.

Autoscaling needs realistic startup time, quotas and warm capacity. Test long requests, bursts, adapter churn and node failure. A fallback model/provider must meet the same data and task-quality requirements. Self-hosting changes control and dependency, but total cost includes utilization, operations, evaluation and human review—not just token or GPU price.

Worked case · Stable extraction, changing reference facts

A model extracts a fixed set of fields from documents and sometimes needs current reference facts to interpret them. A test deployment queues long documents. Which problem would you solve with training, and what would you measure before adding GPUs?

  1. Isolate the two requirements. Start with a prompt and an output schema for the fixed fields. Fetch current facts from a versioned source at request time. If the prompt still misses systematic extraction patterns, compare LoRA on labeled examples. Consider QLoRA when base weights constrain training memory, then measure whether extraction quality and memory use meet the same requirements.
  2. Make the comparison reproducible. Keep document families together and reserve a later period for testing if deployment sees future documents. Freeze the split, preprocessing and label definitions. Record the base and tokenizer revisions, adapter weights and configuration, and serving settings. Verify loading and check field-level precision and recall, malformed output, and high-consequence extraction errors.
  3. Follow the queued requests. Each active sequence retains attention state in the KV cache. Longer inputs and outputs increase the number of cached tokens. In a synthetic estimate with capacity for 24,000 cached tokens, four 6,000-token sequences could fill that budget before scheduler limits and overhead. This is a memory bound under equal sequence lengths. A load test with the actual length mix still needs to satisfy latency, throughput and error gates.
  4. Test the bottleneck. Compare queue time, cache usage and time to first token across length groups. If long requests coincide with cache saturation, test shorter inputs or admission limits before assuming compute is slow. Use data-parallel training to split examples across replicas and synchronize gradients. Consider tensor-parallel inference when spreading a single model’s layer computation across GPUs helps it fit or meet latency goals. Device communication adds a cost.

Sample explanation. I’d retrieve the changing facts and keep the prompt baseline for extraction until held-out errors justify training. I’d pin the base and adapter together and require quality and load tests. A growing queue with a full KV cache makes long-sequence capacity the first hypothesis I’d test.

Optional · 5 min. Find the cache token budget and the stated assumption behind its concurrency estimate. vLLM on parallelism and scaling

Closed-book output

Sketch the prompt, retrieval and adaptation choices. Name the compatible adapter artifacts and the evidence that would distinguish cache saturation from slow computation. Explain data-parallel training and tensor-parallel inference in one sentence each.

Why might eight GPUs be slower than one?
Input processing, data loading, synchronization or small batches can dominate. Measure the actual bottleneck under comparable conditions before changing capacity.
What does a better average score hide?
Rare-class regressions, weak sample sizes, leakage and high-consequence failures. Use per-class/boundary evidence and agreed release limits.
What is the right cost metric?
Cost per successful task at the required quality, latency and availability, including retries, idle capacity, evaluation and review.

Production ML and reliability

Event time: when an interaction occurred. Processing time: when the pipeline handles it. Point-in-time correctness: features contain only information available at the decision time. Calibration: predicted probabilities agree with observed frequencies in an evaluated population.

Keep data and metric definitions honest

Trace source, ingestion, feature computation, training join, registry, serving and feedback. A pipeline’s events/second, a feature store’s writes/second and a model’s predictions/second are different measurements. Daily request volume gives an average, not a peak-capacity result. State what was measured, where and over which interval.

Features must contain only information available at prediction time. Outcome labels can mature later; keep examples out of final evaluation until their observation window is complete. Delayed conversion labels can make recent examples appear negative too soon. Time/entity splits and data availability checks reduce leakage. Monitor model errors by meaningful group as well as overall quality.

Handle late, repeated and stale information

Choose event-time windows and allowed lateness from the freshness requirement. Define correction behavior, deduplication and retry-safe sinks. Separate missing data from a valid zero. Track backlog, processing lag, feature age, invalid events and downstream materialization. Processing guarantees inside a framework do not prove end-to-end exactly-once behavior.

Use shadow traffic to compare a new model without using its decision; it still consumes capacity and can expose request data. Canary releases increase live exposure gradually behind quality and reliability gates. A rollback must restore compatible models, features/configuration and application behavior. Two regions require tested health detection, routing and surviving capacity; deployment count does not establish recovery time.

Worked case · The API is healthy and the features are stale

A recommendation model scores a session at 10:05. An interaction happened at 10:00 but reaches the pipeline at 10:08, followed by a duplicate. A conversion may occur any time in the next day. Which data belong in the original training example?

  1. Rebuild what serving could see. The late interaction belongs in the window that includes 10:00 event time. It was absent from the materialized features at 10:05, so exclude it from that prediction’s training features even if the corrected window contains it later. Keep the label pending until the conversion window and ingestion allowance have elapsed. Evaluate on mature examples. For rare conversions, precision measures the share of predicted positives that converted, and recall the share of conversions found. Check calibration if predicted probabilities drive decisions.
  2. Locate and contain the lag. Compare source arrival, pipeline progress, the store’s latest successful updates and the age or missing rate of features read by serving. Compare by entity or partition to find the affected scope and interval. Count pipeline events/second, store writes/second and inference requests/second at their own boundaries. If serving is reading features past the allowed age, route affected traffic to an approved fallback or suspend those decisions.
  3. Recover with compatible state. Deduplicate by stable event identity so a replay cannot double count. Apply late corrections to the affected window under a defined policy, and verify replay leaves the intended store value unchanged. Check fresh reads and decision coverage before restoring traffic. If reverting a release, restore a compatible application, feature configuration and model together. Confirm the data has also caught up.

Sample explanation. I’d rebuild the feature vector from what the store could serve at 10:05 and set the conversion label after its window matures. During the incident I’d contain stale reads, confirm the replay counts each event once and verify freshness at serving before restoring the affected traffic.

Optional · 5 min. Trace an out-of-order event through a window and compare event time with processing time. Apache Beam on watermarks and late data

Closed-book output

Draw source, pipeline, store and serving. Mark where the late event and duplicate belong, who owns containment and how you would verify replay and restored freshness. Identify the affected interval using serving evidence.

What should the dashboards show?
System health, data freshness/completeness, model quality/coverage and decision outcomes. Green infrastructure does not establish useful predictions.
What is a safe response to stale features?
Quantify exposure, locate the stalled stage and use a documented fallback or containment decision appropriate to risk. Verify freshness and coverage after recovery.
How should regional resilience be described?
Explain health checks, routing, capacity and failure testing. State measured recovery separately from targets and assumptions.

Finish by naming five metrics from memory and defending one containment, recovery and verification sequence.

People leadership

Be clear about responsibility

Distinguish personal implementation, architecture/delivery leadership and formal management authority. Explain team and partner contributions without inflating reporting lines, budget ownership or appraisal responsibility. A strong example shows a decision and its consequence, not a more senior verb attached to a task.

Delegate an outcome; provide support

Agree the desired result, owner, constraints, interfaces and review checkpoints. Let engineers make implementation decisions within those boundaries. Review high-risk architecture and releases without making every small change depend on one person. Build maintainers and operating knowledge, particularly when a project began with one developer.

Open this topic’s diagram.

Handle poor performance fairly

Start with specific examples and a private conversation. Check whether expectations, priorities and resources were clear; listen before deciding the cause. Separate skill gaps, capacity, dependencies, role clarity and conduct. Agree observable improvement, support and review dates, document fairly and follow the company’s process with the accountable manager and HR. Do not speculate about personal circumstances or promise an arbitrary dismissal timetable.

For stakeholder disagreement, agree the outcome and constraints, compare alternatives using the same evidence and make decision rights explicit. Explain trade-offs in value, risk, cost and delivery. Do not leave an engineer alone to negotiate a commitment imposed by a senior stakeholder. Record the decision and what evidence would justify revisiting it.

Worked case · An unfamiliar dependency threatens a milestone

A committed milestone depends on an unfamiliar component exposed by a partner team. The engineer building the integration is blocked by an untested interface. What should the engineer own, and which decision belongs to the stakeholder?

  1. Set task and decision boundaries. The engineer owns a narrow end-to-end prototype and proposes implementation choices. The lead supplies a partner contact and pairing time. Review data contracts and release risk together. The partner owns its interface commitment, and the delivery stakeholder owns the scope/date decision.
  2. Choose an observable checkpoint. Agree to bring a passing test for one supported interface path, or a concrete blocker, to the next checkpoint. Agree when to raise a missing partner response. A checkpoint should give enough time to make a scope decision before the milestone slips.
  3. Put options in front of the decision owner. If the interface remains unsettled, keep the date with a smaller proven scope or move the date to deliver the intended scope. Describe lost value, remaining risk and affected dependencies for each. Ask the accountable stakeholder to choose and record the decision and next review point.

Hypothetical coaching dialogue. Lead “What’s the smallest test that would tell us the interface works?” Engineer “One supported record through the partner sandbox. I need them to confirm which fields are stable.” Lead “You own the test. I’ll arrange a joint session. Bring the result and the blocker to our checkpoint so I can take the scope options to the sponsor.”

Closed-book output · Two project stories

Tell one coaching or delegation story and one prioritization or disagreement story. Follow the same sequence for each. Context → your decision and action → others’ contribution → observed evidence → limitation or lesson. Use an explicitly hypothetical approach wherever you lack a real example.

How can a manager remain technically credible?
Understand the architecture, review consequential decisions and ask evidence-based questions. Develop engineers’ judgment rather than take back all implementation.
What if a strong engineer harms collaboration?
Discuss specific behavior and delivery impact, set clear expectations and support change. Technical output does not exempt someone from team standards.
What if the business demands a date that skips a required gate?
Show safe scope/date options, involve accountable control owners and document the decision. Reduce scope or delay rather than silently bypass required checks.

Finish by telling both stories without notes and answering who owned the decision, what changed and how you know.

Healthcare quality and software assurance

GxP: a family of good-practice regimes, including manufacturing, clinical and laboratory practices. QMS: quality management system. Intended use: who uses a function, for which decision and with what consequences. Assurance: objective evidence that a configured system is fit for that use.

Classify the function before choosing controls

A general office assistant, production/quality-workflow support and a patient-specific device/clinical function have different applicability questions. An internal platform label does not settle them. Establish the activity, reliance, records, markets and failure consequences with Quality and Regulatory. Existing software-delivery controls are useful foundations, not proof of regulated validation.

Know the important distinctions

  • QMSR: FDA’s Quality Management System Regulation took effect on 2 February 2026 and incorporates ISO 13485:2016 by reference, with FDA-specific provisions. This is not a platform certification.
  • Software assurance: FDA’s 3 February 2026 nonbinding guidance addresses production and quality-management-system software. Intended use and failure consequences guide assurance effort; it is not a universal validation recipe for every AI tool.
  • Part 11: applicability depends on electronic records/signatures and underlying recordkeeping requirements, not simply on using a database.
  • ALCOA+: attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate; also complete, consistent, enduring and available. Preserve authoritative records and necessary context, not just convenient logs.

Manage a model change as a behavior change

Agree requirements, failure modes and acceptance criteria. Version model, prompts, tools, data/index revisions and policy; test relevant cases; record results, deviations and approval. A vendor upgrade can change behavior without a code change. Maintain a cleared fallback or human process when the approved configuration becomes unavailable. Critical holds and authorization belong outside the model.

Worked case · The average passes and a critical case fails

A procedure assistant returns an obsolete revision during release testing. Average evaluation still passes. How should intended use affect testing and the release decision?

  1. Identify reliance. In the example, operators consult the result for a production step, so a wrong revision can influence their work. Ask the process owner, Quality and Regulatory to confirm intended use, applicable requirements, consequences and assurance depth.
  2. Define evidence for the critical failure. Require current, effective and permitted revisions. Tests should include a withdrawn revision still present in the index, a changed user permission and unavailable source verification. Check retrieved context, the displayed revision and the response when the policy check fails. Record results against the exact configuration under review.
  3. Hold the release and preserve the process. A failed critical case blocks promotion under the agreed criteria. Engineering repairs revision validation and retests the affected paths. The designated release authority reviews the evidence with Quality under the approved process. Use the authoritative, human-run procedure workflow if the assistant cannot verify the approved revision.

Sample explanation. I’d hold promotion because a user relies on the approved instruction for a production step. I’d require evidence that obsolete and unauthorized revisions stay out of both the model context and the response, then follow the release owner’s review process with Quality.

Optional · 5 min. Skim Section V.A on intended use and the risk-based approach. FDA computer software assurance guidance

Closed-book output

Write four lines for one critical case covering the requirement, failure consequence, test evidence and accountable approval. State which fallback the operator uses and what you would ask Quality or Regulatory to determine.

Does an audit trail make a system compliant?
No. Its content, protection, retention and review must meet the applicable workflow and record requirements; governance and assurance extend beyond logging.
Does a high average score justify release?
No. Assess critical failure consequences and agreed scenario-level requirements. An aggregate improvement can hide an unacceptable error.
Who decides applicability?
The accountable process, Quality/Regulatory, legal/privacy and engineering specialists establish the requirements together. Do not claim certification from a generic checklist.

Two-minute answer template

Use the time cues to rehearse a real project or an explicitly hypothetical design.

  1. 0–25 seconds. State the problem, stakes, what you owned and what others supplied.
  2. 25–65 seconds. Explain the mechanism by tracing an input, decision and output.
  3. 65–90 seconds. Name an alternative, its trade-off and one failure with its recovery path.
  4. 90–120 seconds. Give the evidence, its limits and what you would verify next.

Five checks for each answer

  • Ownership. Could the listener tell which decisions and work were yours and which were the team’s?
  • Mechanism. Could they trace a request, event or decision through the system?
  • Trade-off. Did you name a credible alternative and explain what tipped the decision?
  • Failure. Did you describe a concrete failure, how to detect it and what to do next?
  • Evidence. Did you state what was observed, what remains uncertain and what the measurement covers?

Use the five checks as a practice rubric. The review marks record completed review.

Project revision map and concept refreshers

Pick a project you actually worked on and redraw the part you can explain in depth. Mark what shipped, what you evaluated and what you would propose next. Use observed evidence for the implementation and name the assumptions behind any proposed extension.

Incident investigation
Redraw the request, tool gate, evidence and verdict. Revise correlation and runtime provenance. What ties a log or metric to the affected revision? What evidence would change the conclusion?
LLM adaptation and serving
Redraw the split, adapter artifact and request queue. Revise memory versus compute bottlenecks. What has to match at load time? How would you tell cache pressure from slow decoding?
Recommendation or streaming fraud
Redraw event timestamps, feature state, model decision and feedback. Revise point-in-time joins and delayed labels. Which event could leak future information? How would you verify a replay?
GPU anomaly detection
Redraw input, trained model, score and review queue. Revise reconstruction error and thresholds. How could abnormal training records hide future anomalies? Which false alarms can reviewers handle?
Computer-vision pipeline
Redraw ingestion, annotation, split, training and evaluation. Revise class imbalance and group leakage. Which frames share an underlying source? Which rare class could fail while aggregate accuracy rises?
Delivery and mentoring
Redraw responsibilities, partner dependencies and milestones. Revise coaching and decision rights. What changed after your intervention? What evidence belongs to others’ work?

Three concepts to refresh before a deep dive

  • Autoencoder anomaly scores. An autoencoder learns to reconstruct inputs. An unusually large difference between input and reconstruction can flag a review candidate. Contaminated training data can teach it common anomalies, while overlapping train and test records inflate evaluation. Choose the threshold on separate validation data using missed anomalies and review load, then check once on held-out data.
  • Vision evaluation. Keep related frames or images from the same camera session, object or subject in one split to reduce leakage. For each class, recall is the share of its true examples found and precision is the share of its predictions that are correct. Report rare-class results and sample sizes alongside aggregate metrics.
  • Distributed bottlenecks. Compare GPU time spent computing, waiting for input and exchanging gradients. More replicas may add little when data delivery is slow. If a training step exceeds device memory, reduce per-device batch size or test a method that splits model or optimizer state. Confirm end-to-end throughput and evaluation quality after the change.
Multi-agent investigation: a payment failed

A trace identifier links spans of one distributed request, not necessarily a whole shopping journey. Establish permitted customer/order/payment/session identifiers and the time window. A coordinator can assign bounded journey, telemetry and deployment-change investigations, while a payment adapter reads authoritative transaction metadata.

Each specialist returns sources, time range, identifiers, observations and uncertainty. Reconcile contradictions rather than vote on truth. A frontend timeout can coexist with a successful charge; check authoritative status and idempotency before recommending retry. Do not grant automatic recharge/refund authority to an investigation agent.

Follow-up: when are several agents justified? When their access, context or task separation improves quality enough to offset coordination and recovery costs. Compare with a single agent using parallel tools.

Spark, lakehouse and unfamiliar platform fundamentals

A partition is a task-sized share of data; a shuffle moves records between workers for operations such as joins or grouping. Skew creates a few disproportionately slow tasks. Inspect plans and stage metrics before enlarging a cluster. Filter/select early, examine small files and key distribution, and broadcast only a safely small join side.

A lakehouse combines table/management capabilities with shared analytical storage. A managed platform adds governance and operations but does not guarantee good data, useful permissions or efficient execution. Explain transferable engineering principles and distinguish understanding from hands-on experience.

Follow-up: what explains a job slowing after adding workers? Input/storage limits, shuffle overhead, skew, memory pressure or task sizes may dominate. Compare the same workload and inspect evidence.

GPU memory, distributed training and model evaluation

Separate weight memory, activations, optimizer state and serving KV cache. Data parallelism, tensor/model parallelism and state sharding address different constraints. Synchronization, storage and preprocessing can limit scale. Check gradient accumulation, data sharding, checkpoint consistency and evaluation comparability.

For imbalanced problems, inspect rare-class precision/recall and false-positive cost. Choose appropriate time/entity splits. Accuracy can hide failure in a small important class. Sampling uncertainty and domain-reviewed boundary cases matter even when the headline result improves.

Follow-up: does high GPU utilization prove useful performance? No. Measure examples/tokens per second, end-to-end time and quality under a comparable workload; busy compute can still be wasted.

Alternate project examples: anomaly detection, vision and optimization

An autoencoder can learn normal behavior and expose unusual reconstruction patterns. Anomaly scores are investigation leads, not proof of compromise. Discuss contaminated training data, drift, cold starts, thresholds and analyst workload.

For vision, explain data ingestion, class balance, leakage by entity/time, distributed training, checkpoint evaluation and serving. For optimization, explain variables, objective, hard/soft constraints and how planners use the result. Keep proof-of-concept results separate from production outcomes.

Practice: choose one genuine alternate project. Explain its problem, your decision, others’ contribution, evaluation, operational constraints and supported outcome in three minutes.

First 90 days and cost governance

First month: understand users, intended use, existing systems, team skills and control owners; select a bounded pilot with a baseline. Second: implement and test the supported delivery path, documentation and fallback. Third: roll out only if acceptance/assurance criteria are met; measure adoption, usefulness and operating burden. These are planning stages, not a promise of regulatory approval in 90 days.

Compare costs across model calls, hosting, storage, retrieval, evaluation, support and human review. Allocate usage by application/environment. Use quotas, bounded retries and context limits, and evaluate cost per useful task. A cheaper model can cost more overall when it increases rework.
